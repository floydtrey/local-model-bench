# Law

This document is the authoritative law book for governed systems. It defines authority, prohibitions, requirements, exceptions, and the process for changing those rules.

Intent does not create legal authority. State does not create legal authority. Historical records, audits, plans, task decisions, model outputs, and implementation details do not create legal authority unless this Law expressly gives them that effect.

# Part 1 — General Provisions

## Subpart A — Authority and Applicability

### § 1.1 Source and scope of governing authority

(a) Every action performed by a governed actor while acting within a governed context is subject to this Law.

(b) This Law may authorize actions individually or by defined class. Where a class of actions is authorized, an individual action within that class does not require a separate provision solely because the specific action is not named.

(c) Authority granted by a class remains limited by the scope, conditions, restrictions, and boundaries applicable to that class and to the actor performing the action.

(d) This Law may restrict, condition, or prohibit authority otherwise available through a task, system, tool, role, capability, or other authorized mechanism.

(e) The absence of an express prohibition does not create authority outside an applicable grant or delegated class of authority.

(f) Intent may guide the objective and desired outcome of work, but Intent does not create legal authority and may not authorize an action contrary to this Law.

(g) State records only State-eligible shared facts and effective authorized conditions under § 1.12. State does not amend, supersede, waive, or override this Law.

(h) For purposes of this Law, a governed actor is a human principal, model, agent, service, application, or other actor performing work within a governed context.

(i) A governed context is a project, system, role, session, task, environment, or interaction to which this Law has been made applicable by Owner adoption, lawful incorporation, or another applicability rule established by this Law.

(j) Mere possession, storage, retrieval, or visibility of this Law does not by itself make an otherwise unrelated actor or context governed.

### § 1.2 Owner authority

(a) The Owner is the final human authority over this Law.

(b) Only the authenticated Owner may approve:
(1) adoption of this Law;
(2) amendment or repeal of this Law or any provision;
(3) an exception requiring Owner approval under this Law; or
(4) an explicit Owner override.

(c) Human status, account access, administrative access, possession of a device or credential, or authority in another role does not by itself confer Owner authority.

(d) No principal other than the authenticated Owner may exercise Owner authority unless this Law is expressly amended to provide otherwise.

(e) Owner authority must not be inferred from tone, conversational context, apparent urgency, prior behavior, or a representation made by another principal.

(f) Owner authority may be exercised only by the principal whose Owner identity is established through an authentication or identity mechanism recognized under this Law and identified through authoritative State or another authoritative source expressly recognized by this Law.

(g) The Owner identity designation is a protected decision. No actor may infer Owner identity from a name, account ownership, repository administration, device possession, conversational history, prior access, or similar circumstantial evidence.

(h) If the current Owner identity cannot be reliably authenticated through the recognized mechanism, an Owner-only action must not proceed until Owner identity is re-established through that mechanism. A model or other actor may not resolve the failure by selecting or accepting a substitute Owner.

### § 1.3 Delegated authority

(a) Delegated authority is limited to the actor, action, subject, scope, conditions, and duration established by the applicable authority.

(b) Authority granted to one actor does not automatically transfer to another actor.

(c) Authority to perform one action does not imply authority to perform a related action.

(d) Authority delegated to an actor may be narrower than the authority held by the delegating principal.

(e) An actor may not redelegate its authority unless the applicable Law expressly permits that redelegation.

(f) Authority to propose, draft, inspect, recommend, review, plan, or prepare an action does not by itself include authority to approve, enact, execute, or expand that action.

(g) An actor whose authorized role includes planning, coordination, or task decomposition may assign a bounded subtask to another governed actor when the subtask remains within the delegating actor's existing authorized objective and boundaries, the recipient is authorized for the role, access, capabilities, and resources required, and the assignment does not enlarge authority, bypass a protected decision, or authorize an action that otherwise requires separate authority. Authority passed through such an assignment is no broader than the authority necessary to perform the bounded subtask and remains subject to all applicable Law.

### § 1.4 Routine task authority

(a) The authorized assignment of a bounded task delegates authority to perform the individual actions reasonably required to complete that task without separate approval for each action, when those actions remain within the authority envelope established by this section and all other applicable Law.

(b) An action remains within routine task authority only when:
(1) it directly serves the assigned objective;
(2) it remains within the assigned project, workspace, systems, information, and other applicable task boundaries;
(3) it uses only access, capabilities, tools, and resources already authorized to the actor;
(4) it does not enlarge the actor's privileges, role, authority, or access;
(5) it does not materially expand or replace the assigned objective;
(6) it does not modify Law or Intent unless that class of change is separately authorized;
(7) any State change is limited to maintenance of a State-eligible class under § 1.12 that the actor is authorized to maintain and does not itself create, expand, reduce, transfer, extend, revoke, restore, or otherwise materially affect authority, permissions, protected boundaries, or another governed actor's rights unless that authority-bearing change is separately authorized;
(8) it does not create a new external commitment, publication, deployment, purchase, release, or other consequential external action unless that class of action is separately authorized; and
(9) it does not destroy unique or unrecoverable information or create an irreversible consequence unless that class of action is separately authorized.

(c) Routine task authority may cover classes of work such as inspection, reading, analysis, drafting, coding, editing, testing, building, searching, and tool use when the particular action satisfies paragraph (b). These examples are illustrative and do not create authority outside the stated boundaries.

(d) Possession of a tool, credential, capability, filesystem path, API, account, or technical ability does not itself create authority to use it. Tool use is authorized only to the extent that the intended use falls within an applicable grant of authority and complies with all applicable restrictions.

(e) Routine status is determined by the characteristics and effects of the action, not by how common, familiar, convenient, or technically simple the action appears and not by the name of the tool used.

(f) Within the authorized objective and boundaries, an actor may choose implementation means and take incidental steps needed to complete the work without seeking approval for each choice. This discretion does not authorize material scope expansion, creation of a materially broader objective, or a separately controlled action.

(g) If a proposed action would cross a boundary in paragraph (b), only that action and work materially dependent upon it lose routine authority. Independently authorized work may continue.

(h) State may record active authority, exceptions, overrides, suspensions, assignments, or other authority-relevant conditions only when that authority already exists through this Law, a valid exception under § 1.8, an Owner override under § 1.9, or another grant expressly authorized by this Law. Recording such information in State does not create the authority it describes.

### § 1.5 Applicability

(a) A provision applies according to its text, defined terms, stated scope, and any conditions or relationships established by this Law.

(b) Where a provision authorizes or governs a defined class of actors, actions, systems, information, or circumstances, it applies to an individual case that satisfies the defining characteristics of that class. The individual case need not be named separately.

(c) Similarity alone does not establish membership in a governed class. The material characteristics required by the applicable provision must be satisfied.

(d) Task context and State may establish facts necessary to determine whether a provision applies. Neither may enlarge the scope of the provision or create applicability that the Law does not support.

(e) If a material fact necessary to determine applicability is unresolved, the dependent action must not be treated as authorized until the fact is established through an authorized source or the matter is resolved through the applicable escalation process.

(f) Where multiple provisions apply, they must be given effect together when their requirements are compatible.

(g) A model or other interpreter must not invent precedence between conflicting provisions based solely on specificity, recency, document order, citation number, retrieval rank, frequency of past use, or perceived importance.

(h) Where applicable provisions materially conflict and this Law does not establish their relationship or precedence, the dependent action must be treated as unresolved and elevated for Owner resolution.

(i) An unresolved applicability issue blocks only the action and other work materially dependent upon it. Independently authorized work may continue.

## Subpart B — Interpretation and Decision

### § 1.6 Determination of governing authority

(a) A Governor, arbiter, model, or other authorized interpreter may determine how this Law applies to a proposed action but may not create, expand, waive, amend, or invent authority.

(b) A determination must be based on:
(1) the current effective Law;
(2) any valid effective exception applicable to the action;
(3) any explicit effective Owner override applicable to the action;
(4) relevant State facts; and
(5) the authorized task context and other facts necessary to determine the action, actor, scope, conditions, and applicable authority.

(c) Before treating a matter as unresolved, the interpreter may use authorized sources to establish material facts that can be determined without changing authority or policy. Failure to know a fact is not itself a reason to elevate when that fact can be reliably established through already authorized means.

(d) A governing-authority determination must resolve the proposed action as authorized, denied, or unresolved.

(e) An action is authorized only when an applicable grant of authority covers the action and all material conditions and restrictions required for that authority are satisfied.

(f) Where authority applies subject to conditions or restrictions, those conditions and restrictions remain part of the authorization. An interpreter may not omit them merely because the underlying action is otherwise permitted.

(g) An action is denied when the material facts and applicable Law are sufficiently established and:
(1) applicable Law expressly prohibits the action and no valid applicable exception or Owner override permits it; or
(2) no applicable grant of authority covers the action.

(h) A denial under paragraph (g) is a determination that the requested action lacks current authority. The absence of authority must not be treated as ambiguity merely because the requester prefers a different result.

(i) A matter is unresolved only when a reliable authorization or denial cannot be determined because:
(1) a material fact necessary to determine authority cannot be reliably established;
(2) applicable provisions materially conflict without a controlling relationship established by Law;
(3) the meaning or applicability of controlling Law remains materially ambiguous; or
(4) the current effective Law or another required authoritative source cannot be reliably established.

(j) An unresolved action must not be treated as authorized merely because it is not expressly prohibited. A denied action must not be treated as unresolved merely to seek a more favorable interpretation.

(k) Where a matter remains unresolved after available authorized facts have been established, the dependent action must not proceed and the matter must be elevated to the Owner or to another authority expressly designated by this Law. Independently authorized work remains governed by § 1.5(i).

(l) A denied action does not require elevation merely because it was denied. If departure from the controlling Law is sought, the request must proceed through a valid exception under § 1.8, an Owner override under § 1.9, an amendment under § 1.10, or another lawful authority expressly provided by this Law.

(m) Intent, plans, objectives, desired outcomes, historical practice, implementation convenience, anticipated benefit, model preference, or previous authority determinations must not alter the meaning of Law or create authority.

(n) A prior Governor, arbiter, model, or human interpretation does not become Law or binding precedent merely because it was previously relied upon, recorded, repeated, or successful. Only authority established under this Law controls subsequent determinations.

(o) The governing basis for a material authorization, denial, restriction, unresolved determination, or escalation must be identifiable and preserved sufficiently for the determination to be checked against the applicable authority.

(p) A governing-authority determination must preserve enough information to identify the decision and its legal basis. No particular model response format, schema, wording, or serialization is required. Where an authorized deterministic mechanism exists and can reliably perform representation, validation, association, or recordkeeping without model judgment, that mechanism should be used in preference to requiring the model to perform the same function. Absence of such a mechanism does not by itself prohibit otherwise authorized work.

### § 1.7 Protected decisions and delegated discretion

(a) Except where this Law reserves a decision to an authoritative source, an actor acting within routine task authority may exercise reasonable discretion in selecting implementation means not otherwise prescribed by Law, applicable Intent, task conditions, or authoritative State.

(b) Routine implementation discretion may include consequential technical or engineering choices when the choice remains within the authorized objective and boundaries, is reasonably reversible, and is not otherwise reserved by Law. A choice does not become protected merely because an incorrect implementation could reduce quality, performance, efficiency, or reliability.

(c) A decision is protected where this Law expressly requires an authoritative value, class, approval, or assignment, or where making the selection would itself create or materially alter authority, permissions, access rights, a protected security boundary, an external commitment, an irreversible or destructive consequence, or project direction beyond the assigned objective.

(d) An actor making a protected decision must establish the required authoritative value or permitted class from the source designated by Law. Technical capability, convenience, similarity, prior use, model preference, or the absence of an express prohibition does not authorize substitution.

(e) If the required authoritative value is missing, conflicting, unreadable, invalid, outside the class permitted by Law, or cannot be reliably established, the dependent action must not proceed. The matter must be elevated to the Owner or to another authority expressly designated by this Law.

(f) Elevation under paragraph (e) applies to the protected decision and to work materially dependent upon it. Independently authorized work may continue.

(g) Selection of a model assigned to a protected governed role is a protected decision. This Law establishes the authority and constraints governing the assignment; the authenticated Owner, or another authority expressly designated by this Law, may establish or change the current permitted model or model class for the role. State records the current approved role-to-model assignment and any material constraint necessary to apply it. Changing the assignment does not require amendment of this Law unless the change would alter a constraint established by Law.

(h) If a required role-to-model assignment is absent, conflicting, invalid, outside an applicable Law-established constraint, or cannot be reliably established from State, the assignment must be elevated under paragraph (e). No actor may infer, substitute, downgrade, default to, or otherwise select another model on its own authority.

## Subpart C — Exceptions and Overrides

### § 1.8 Owner-authorized exceptions

(a) The Owner may authorize a limited exception to a provision or governed boundary only where this Law permits an exception to that provision or boundary. Where no exception is permitted, departure from the Law requires an Owner override under § 1.9 or an amendment under § 1.10.

(b) A valid exception must have an identifiable:
(1) provision or governed boundary affected;
(2) actor, system, project, action, information, resource, or other scope to which the exception applies;
(3) authority, restriction, or boundary being changed for that scope;
(4) conditions and limitations applicable to the exception;
(5) effective start and expiration, completion, revocation, or other termination condition; and
(6) explicit Owner approval.

(c) An exception grants only the departure expressly authorized. It does not create additional authority by implication, analogy, similarity, necessity, convenience, or technical capability.

(d) An exception does not amend the underlying Law. Except within the authorized exception's scope and effective conditions, the underlying Law remains fully applicable.

(e) Owner approval under this section is the source of the exceptional authority. State records whether the approved exception is currently effective and the material facts necessary to apply it. Recording an exception in State does not itself create or enlarge the authority.

(f) An approved exception must be represented in current State before a governed system relies upon it operationally. The representation must preserve enough information to establish the exception's identity, scope, conditions, current effectiveness, and Owner authorization. This paragraph does not require a particular document format, schema, field set, or storage implementation.

(g) State must not represent an exception as active before its effective conditions are satisfied or after it expires, is completed, is revoked, or otherwise ceases to be effective.

(h) Expired, revoked, completed, superseded, or otherwise inactive exceptions may be preserved as history or evidence, but they have no current authority.

(i) The Owner may revoke an exception without amending the underlying Law. Revocation ends only the authority granted by that exception unless the Owner expressly directs another lawful effect.

(j) No non-Owner principal may create, approve, expand, extend, restore, or reactivate an exception unless this Law is amended to delegate that specific authority.

### § 1.9 Owner override

(a) The Owner may explicitly authorize an action notwithstanding a conflicting provision of this Law.

(b) An Owner override is an exceptional exercise of final Owner authority. It is not an exception under § 1.8, an interpretation of the conflicting provision, or an amendment to this Law.

(c) Before relying on an override, the material conflict with Law must be identified to the Owner sufficiently to distinguish an informed override from ordinary task approval or instruction.

(d) A valid override must identify the action or authority being permitted and the provision or governed boundary being overridden with enough specificity to determine the scope of the departure.

(e) An override applies only to the action, actor, project, system, information, resource, conditions, duration, or other scope expressly established by the Owner. Authority must not be expanded from an override by implication, analogy, similarity, convenience, necessity, or prior use.

(f) A general instruction to complete a task, solve a problem, use available tools, proceed despite difficulty, or achieve an objective does not by itself constitute an override of conflicting Law.

(g) A model, arbiter, worker, system, or other non-Owner principal may identify that an override would be required, but may not infer, manufacture, approve, or exercise an Owner override.

(h) A one-time override may be consumed by the specifically authorized action and preserved with the authority determination and execution evidence. An override intended to remain effective for future or repeated reliance must be represented in current State before such reliance occurs.

(i) State records the current effectiveness and material conditions of a continuing override but does not create the override. An inactive, expired, completed, revoked, or consumed override has no current authority.

(j) An Owner override does not amend, repeal, reinterpret, or create precedent under the underlying Law. The conflicting Law continues to govern all matters outside the override's valid scope.

(k) An override changes governing authority only. It does not create technical capability, credentials, access, factual conditions, or authority controlled by an external system or jurisdiction.

(l) Repeated or similar Owner overrides do not amend this Law or create continuing authority by repetition. Where the Owner intends the departure from Law to become the normal governing rule rather than a bounded departure, that change must be made by amendment under § 1.10.

## Subpart D — Changes to Law

### § 1.10 Amendment authority

(a) Only the authenticated Owner may approve adoption, amendment, repeal, redesignation, or reservation of a provision of this Law.

(b) Any governed actor may identify a defect, conflict, omission, ambiguity, or possible improvement and may propose an amendment when authorized to do so. A proposal, draft, recommendation, review, audit finding, or model-generated revision has no legal effect by itself.

(c) An amendment must identify with sufficient precision:
(1) the provision or provisions affected;
(2) whether each affected provision is being added, revised, repealed, redesignated, or reserved;
(3) the resulting legal effect or resulting text; and
(4) any approved condition governing when the amendment becomes effective.

(d) Owner approval must apply to the amendment actually being enacted. Approval of an objective, concept, task, plan, discussion, implementation, or earlier draft does not by itself approve materially different Law text later produced from it.

(e) Where the Owner gives an unambiguous instruction that itself establishes the exact legal change without requiring material drafting discretion, that instruction may constitute approval of the amendment. Where material wording, scope, conditions, or legal effect are subsequently developed or changed, the resulting amendment requires Owner approval before becoming Law.

(f) Owner approval does not require prescribed words, a particular response format, a schema, or a ceremonial confirmation. The approval and the amendment to which it applies must nevertheless be identifiable and attributable to the authenticated Owner.

(g) Unless the Owner expressly approves a broader effect, an amendment changes only the identified provisions and does not silently alter neighboring provisions, definitions, exceptions, authority relationships, or other Law.

(h) Where multiple changes are approved as one amendment and their legal effect depends upon one another, they must take effect as one governing unit unless the Owner expressly authorizes a separable effect. A failed or incomplete application must not leave a materially different partial Law in force.

(i) Technical ability or repository authority to modify `LAW.md` does not itself confer authority to amend Law. An unapproved file edit, merge, synchronization, migration, model output, or other technical change has no legal effect and must not be represented as effective Law.

(j) Amendment implementation may be handled by deterministic software or other authorized mechanisms. This Law governs the required authority and legal effect; it does not prescribe a particular editing, storage, version-control, or serialization mechanism.

(k) A proposed or approved amendment does not alter current Law until it becomes effective under § 1.11.

### § 1.11 Effectiveness and history

(a) Draft, proposed, pending, rejected, or unapproved provisions have no governing authority.

(b) An amendment becomes effective only when:
(1) the amendment has been approved under § 1.10;
(2) the exact approved legal change has been incorporated into the current authoritative Law through an authorized mechanism; and
(3) any approved effective time, event, condition, or other prerequisite has been satisfied.

(c) Owner approval authorizes the amendment but does not require a governed system to treat text that has not yet been successfully incorporated into the authoritative Law as current operative Law.

(d) Unless the Owner expressly establishes a later effective condition, an approved amendment may become effective upon successful authorized incorporation into the authoritative Law.

(e) A future-effective amendment must remain distinguishable from current operative Law until its effective condition is satisfied. Its approval or presence in a repository does not make it prematurely effective.

(f) An amendment applies prospectively unless the Owner expressly approves a retrospective effect and its scope. A later amendment must not silently change the legal standing of actions completed under the Law effective at the time.

(g) Repeal, expiration, supersession, or replacement ends the current authority of the affected provision according to the approved legal change. Removal of a provision does not revive an earlier provision unless the approved amendment expressly provides for that result.

(h) Historical versions of Law must be preserved or otherwise recoverable sufficiently to determine what Law was effective at a material prior time. Historical Law may be used to evaluate past actions but must not be represented as current authority.

(i) Current effective Law must remain distinguishable from drafts, pending amendments, superseded text, historical editions, and other non-effective material.

(j) If the current effective Law or the effective status of a material provision cannot be reliably established, an actor must not select among competing versions by recency, repository order, apparent completeness, model judgment, or other inference. The dependent action must be treated as unresolved and elevated under § 1.6.

(k) A technical failure that prevents an approved amendment from being completely and correctly incorporated must not create a partial or uncertain governing state. Where § 1.10(h) requires changes to take effect as one governing unit, the prior effective Law remains controlling unless and until the approved amendment is successfully incorporated as authorized.

(l) Preservation of legal history does not require a particular repository, version-control product, database, file layout, schema, or storage mechanism.

## Subpart E — State and Intent

### § 1.12 State scope and freshness

(a) State is a small authoritative register of shared current facts and lawful conditions that governed actors may need across projects or systems and that cannot safely be left to inference.

(b) Information is eligible for State only when it falls within a class designated by this section or another provision of this Law and when maintaining one authoritative current value is materially useful to governed work.

(c) State-eligible classes include:
(1) Owner identity or binding information required by § 1.2, excluding secret authentication material;
(2) protected role-to-model assignments required by § 1.7;
(3) active exceptions under § 1.8;
(4) continuing Owner overrides under § 1.9;
(5) durable shared environment and hardware facts that may be unavailable to a governed actor through reliable direct inspection;
(6) stable infrastructure addressing and topology, including host identities, configured endpoints, ports, canonical install roots, shared paths, and durable service relationships;
(7) Law-referenced variables, approved sets, assignments, or configuration values whose legal meaning is established by Law but whose current value is intended to change without amending the surrounding provision; and
(8) other cross-project or system-wide facts expressly designated for State by this Law.

(d) A State-eligible fact must be sufficiently durable that maintaining it as authoritative current State is reasonably practicable. A fact that changes frequently enough that the State record would predictably become stale must not be placed in State merely for convenience. This durability limitation does not exclude an authority-bearing entry that this Law expressly requires to be represented in State for the duration of its lawful effect.

(e) Project-specific knowledge, task progress, strategy, audit findings, diagnostic records, implementation history, transient runtime state, service health, current process state, temporary debugging configuration, and other frequently changing operational information are not eligible for State unless another provision of this Law expressly designates the specific class as State-eligible.

(f) Credentials, passwords, tokens, private keys, secret values, and equivalent authentication material must not be stored in State. State may identify the authoritative credential source, credential class, or binding needed by governed systems without containing the secret itself.

(g) A configured host, endpoint, port, path, or other infrastructure fact may be recorded in State even though its live operational condition must be established elsewhere. Recording the configured value in State does not establish that the service or resource is currently reachable, healthy, listening, mounted, or otherwise operational.

(h) Presence in State does not make an entry legally valid or create authority. An authority-bearing State entry is valid only to the extent supported by the Law, exception, Owner override, or other lawful authority that created it.

(i) A governed actor may rely on a State value only while the value can reasonably be treated as current for the decision being made. Evidence that a State value is stale, conflicting, invalid, or superseded defeats reliance on that value until its current status is established.

(j) Where State is the authoritative source for a protected decision or required assignment, a missing, conflicting, invalid, or materially stale State value requires mandatory elevation under § 1.7. Inference, substitution, or default selection is not permitted.

(k) Where a non-protected State value appears stale and a current value can be reliably established from an authorized source, an actor authorized to maintain that State class may verify and update the value. If the value cannot be reliably established, it must not be represented as current, and any action materially dependent upon it is unresolved under § 1.6.

(l) Authority to maintain State does not include authority to create, expand, reduce, transfer, extend, revoke, restore, or otherwise materially alter authority, permissions, protected boundaries, exceptions, overrides, or another governed actor's rights unless that authority-bearing change is separately authorized.

(m) State must remain limited to the minimum information necessary to serve the purposes established by this section. Information that belongs in a project, Knowledge Core, a live operational source, or another authoritative system must not be duplicated into State solely to make retrieval more convenient.

### § 1.13 Intent applicability and alignment

(a) Intent establishes project purpose, direction, priorities, and desired outcomes. Intent does not create governing authority and must not amend, waive, override, or reinterpret this Law.

(b) Project Intent applies within the scope of the project to which it belongs. Where applicable Project Intent and General Intent materially conflict, Project Intent controls the project-direction question within that project.

(c) General Intent applies to governed project-direction questions not otherwise resolved by applicable Project Intent.

(d) Neither Project Intent nor General Intent may create authority prohibited or withheld by this Law, validate an otherwise unauthorized action, or alter the legal effect of a provision.

(e) A plan or material task subject to governed planning review must be evaluated against applicable Intent after governing authority has been determined. The review must distinguish legal authority from Intent alignment.

(f) A plan that is legally authorized but materially inconsistent with applicable Intent must not be approved as aligned work merely because the actions are lawful. The plan must be revised, the inconsistency resolved through applicable project authority, or the controlling Intent lawfully changed.

(g) Where applicable Intent is materially ambiguous or conflicting in a manner that could change project direction, the dependent planning decision must not be resolved by inventing preference. The matter must be elevated to the Owner or to another authority expressly designated to resolve that project-direction question.

(h) An Intent-alignment determination must preserve enough information to identify the alignment decision, the applicable Intent, and any material inconsistency relied upon. No particular model response format, schema, score, wording, or serialization is required. Where an authorized deterministic mechanism exists and can reliably perform representation, association, validation, or recordkeeping without model judgment, that mechanism should be used in preference to requiring the model to perform the same function. Absence of such a mechanism does not by itself prohibit otherwise authorized planning or work.

### § 1.14 Intent change authority

(a) General Intent and Project Intent are authoritative statements of project direction only after they have been established or materially changed with Owner approval.

(b) A governed actor may identify ambiguity, conflict, omission, drift, or a possible change in Intent and may propose revised Intent when authorized to do so. A proposal, draft, summary, interpretation, task result, plan, or model-generated revision does not by itself change authoritative Intent.

(c) Only the authenticated Owner may approve a material addition, revision, removal, or replacement of General Intent or Project Intent unless this Law expressly delegates that authority.

(d) Owner approval must apply to the Intent actually being established. Approval of a task, implementation, plan, discussion, example, or earlier draft does not by itself approve materially different Intent later derived from it.

(e) Where the Owner gives an unambiguous instruction that itself establishes the intended project direction without requiring material drafting discretion, that instruction may constitute approval of the corresponding Intent change.

(f) Where material wording, scope, priority, constraint, tradeoff, or project direction is subsequently developed or changed beyond the Owner's approved instruction, the resulting Intent change requires Owner approval before becoming authoritative.

(g) Owner approval of an Intent change does not require prescribed words, a particular response format, schema, or ceremonial confirmation. The approved change and the Intent to which it applies must nevertheless be identifiable and attributable to the authenticated Owner.

(h) An approved Intent change becomes current only after the approved meaning has been incorporated into the applicable authoritative Intent through an authorized mechanism.

(i) A technical edit, repository write, merge, model output, synchronization, or other change to an Intent file does not by itself make that change authoritative.

(j) Historical or superseded Intent may be preserved for provenance or explanation but must remain distinguishable from current authoritative Intent.

(k) A Project Intent change affects only the project to which that Intent applies unless the Owner expressly changes General Intent or another Project Intent separately.

(l) A General Intent change does not silently rewrite existing Project Intent. Where a current Project Intent conflicts with General Intent, § 1.13(b) continues to govern unless the Owner separately changes that Project Intent.

(m) If the current authoritative Intent or the effective status of a material Intent change cannot be reliably established, the dependent project-direction decision must be treated as unresolved and elevated under § 1.13(g).

# Part 10 — Reliability, Versioning, and Diagnostic Evidence

## Subpart A — Version and Change Traceability

### § 10.1 Version and change history

(a) Each governed project must preserve sufficient version and change history to identify the current authoritative version of material project artifacts, distinguish prior material versions, and support investigation or recovery when a material change produces an adverse or uncertain result.

(b) Where a known-working baseline exists and a proposed change could materially disrupt that baseline, the baseline must remain preserved or otherwise recoverable while the proposed replacement or repair is evaluated, unless preservation is not reasonably practicable. The inability to preserve the baseline does not by itself prohibit otherwise authorized work, but the increased recovery risk must be considered before the disruptive change proceeds.

(c) Compliance with this section requires the capability and traceability described in paragraphs (a) and (b). No particular version-control product, repository host, file format, schema, or storage implementation is required.

(d) Version history and change evidence are not State merely because they are current. Their treatment remains subject to § 1.12.

### § 10.2 Material error and failure recording

(a) Each governed project must provide a reliable means of preserving material error and failure evidence sufficient to support later diagnosis, review, and recovery.

(b) Preserved material error or failure evidence must identify the affected operation or context and the observed failure to the extent those facts can be reliably established. Other reasonably available information material to diagnosis or recovery may also be preserved. Benign, expected, transient, or low-value events need not be preserved merely because they occurred.

(c) Error and failure evidence must remain durable enough to permit a later authorized actor to investigate a material failure without relying solely upon conversational memory or recollection.

(d) Compliance with this section requires the capability and result described in paragraphs (a) through (c). No particular logging library, log level, file path, schema, service, storage technology, or user interface is required.

(e) Logging configuration, diagnostic configuration, instrumentation state, and other operational diagnostic details must be obtained from the project or another authoritative operational source when needed and must not be stored in State.

## Subpart B — Failure Evidence and Diagnostic Escalation

### § 10.10 Evidence-driven diagnosis

(a) Before making a repair, a governed actor must consider available diagnostic evidence reasonably capable of identifying the failure or distinguishing among plausible causes. Such evidence may include existing logs, error output, tests, reproducible behavior, current source or configuration, and comparison with a known-working baseline.

(b) A repair should have an evidentiary basis reasonably connecting the proposed change to the observed failure. Failure of a repair is evidence that the diagnosis or proposed remedy may be incomplete or incorrect.

(c) If repeated repair attempts do not establish the cause of a failure, or if successive patches are accumulating without evidence that they address the cause, further speculative modification must stop until diagnostic evidence is increased.

(d) Diagnostic evidence under paragraph (c) may be increased by targeted logging, tracing, assertions, instrumentation, reproduction, variable isolation, comparison with a known-working case, or another proportionate method suited to the unresolved failure.

(e) After diagnostic evidence is increased, the failure must be reproduced or re-examined to the extent reasonably necessary to guide further repair. Diagnostic additions must remain directed at the unresolved cause and must not expand into unrelated infrastructure without separate authority or justification.

(f) No fixed number of attempts or patches is established by this section. Paragraph (c) applies when the cause remains unsupported while speculative repair is repeating or accumulating.

(g) If adequate diagnostic evidence cannot be obtained within the actor's existing authority or task boundaries, the blocked diagnostic need must be elevated. Speculative repair must not continue merely because the required evidence is unavailable.

(h) Evidence sufficient to identify a material failed repair attempt and its relevant result must be preserved when doing so is reasonably practicable and would materially reduce the risk of unnecessary repetition or support later diagnosis. Temporary diagnostics, exploratory patches, and failed approaches do not become permanent architecture, State, or Law merely because they were created during investigation.

# Part 20 — External Content and Data Security

## Subpart A — External Software and Untrusted Content

### § 20.1 External software, code, models, and executable content

(a) Software, source code, models, packages, plugins, scripts, installers, binaries, dependencies, and other content obtained from an external or untrusted source must not be treated as safe solely because it is publicly available, widely referenced, accessible through a trusted platform, or technically compatible with the governed system.

(b) Before externally obtained content is executed, installed, granted access, incorporated into a governed system, or otherwise placed in a position where malicious or defective behavior could create a material effect, reasonable security due diligence proportionate to the expected risk must be performed.

(c) Due diligence under paragraph (b) must consider the evidence reasonably available for the particular source and risk. Relevant evidence may include:
(1) source provenance and identity;
(2) the official or expected distribution location;
(3) available source code or relevant changes;
(4) independent reproduction, testing, review, or community experience;
(5) known security findings, malicious behavior, suspicious installation or execution behavior, and supply-chain concerns;
(6) requested privileges, credentials, filesystem access, network access, persistence, or other capabilities;
(7) whether the content can first be evaluated in a bounded or isolated environment; and
(8) other evidence materially relevant to the foreseeable consequences of use.

(d) The absence of formal certification, a third-party audit, extensive public adoption, or complete source transparency does not by itself prohibit use. Due diligence must be proportionate to the actual exposure, expected benefit, reversibility, available containment, and consequences of a failure or compromise.

(e) Where risk can be materially reduced through reasonable controls, the content may be evaluated or used within those controls when otherwise authorized. Such controls may include isolation, limited privileges, restricted credentials, bounded filesystem or network access, preservation of a known-working baseline, review of relevant code or changes, and staged testing before broader use.

(f) A material unresolved indication of malicious behavior, deceptive provenance, credential theft, unauthorized persistence, destructive behavior, unexplained privilege requirements, or another significant compromise risk must not be disregarded merely to complete the task. The affected use must not proceed while the indication remains materially unresolved unless further investigation or reasonable controls reduce the risk to a level permitted by this section or departure is otherwise lawfully authorized.

(g) Security review under this section must remain proportionate to the proposed use. An actor must not expand a bounded acquisition or evaluation task into an unrelated security project merely because additional hypothetical risks can be imagined.

(h) Downloading or preserving content for inspection does not by itself authorize execution, installation, integration, credential access, deployment, or broader trust. Each materially broader use remains subject to its applicable authority and security requirements.

(i) A prior successful use, clean execution, or absence of observed malicious behavior is supporting evidence but does not establish permanent trust for a different version, source, configuration, or materially different use.

(j) Where the evidence reasonably supports use and applicable risks have been reduced to a level proportionate to the proposed action, the absence of absolute assurance must not by itself prevent otherwise authorized work.

## Subpart B — External Transmission and Publication

### § 20.10 External transmission and uploads

(a) This section governs the outward disclosure of governed information to an external system. Network connectivity or technical communication does not by itself authorize disclosure of governed information.

(b) Communications performed by separately authorized infrastructure software solely within its approved service function, including network transport, remote administration, routing, name resolution, time synchronization, service discovery, or equivalent system operation, are not prohibited by this section merely because the communication crosses the governed-environment boundary. This paragraph does not authorize a governed actor to use such infrastructure as a channel for an otherwise unauthorized disclosure.

(c) A governed actor acting within routine task authority may perform external search, research, discovery, or acquisition requests when the request directly serves the assigned objective and transmits only non-sensitive query terms, identifiers, addresses, protocol information, or other information reasonably necessary to identify, request, or receive the external information or content. This authority does not include uploading project files, source material, logs, private records, credentials, secrets, or other sensitive governed information except as separately authorized by this Law.

(d) If whether a destination is inside or outside the governed environment cannot be reliably established, the destination must be treated as external for purposes of this section.

(e) Credentials, passwords, authentication tokens, private keys, recovery secrets, secret configuration values, or equivalent authentication material must not be transmitted to an external service unless the transmission is specifically required, authorized, and made through a mechanism appropriate for that secret.

(f) Information whose disclosure could materially compromise system security, access control, protected infrastructure, private information, proprietary material, recovery capability, or another protected interest must not be transmitted externally unless the disclosure is authorized and reasonably necessary for the intended purpose.

(g) Where only part of a file, record, dataset, log, source tree, configuration, or other material is necessary for an authorized external disclosure, unnecessary sensitive information must be excluded, redacted, filtered, or otherwise withheld when reasonably practicable.

(h) The use of an approved application, account, cloud service, repository host, model provider, collaboration service, remote-access service, or other external system does not by itself authorize every category of governed information to be disclosed to that system.

(i) Except for communications authorized by paragraphs (b) and (c), a governed actor must not disclose governed information to an external system unless the authenticated Owner has approved the destination or class of disclosure and that approval is represented in current State. Absence of an applicable approved State representation requires the disclosure to fail closed.

(j) Where the sensitivity of information or the authority to disclose it cannot be reliably determined and disclosure could create a material consequence, the disclosure must not proceed until the uncertainty is resolved or elevated.

(k) Automatic synchronization, telemetry, crash reporting, diagnostic upload, backup, remote logging, or similar background transmission is subject to this section when it may disclose governed project information or other protected or materially sensitive information beyond the separately authorized function of the infrastructure or service.

(l) Security review under this section must remain proportionate to the information and proposed destination. The mere possibility that information could be sensitive does not require blocking an otherwise authorized external search, acquisition request, infrastructure communication, or disclosure where the available facts reasonably establish that the transmitted information is suitable for that purpose.

(m) Successful prior transmission to a service does not establish continuing authority to disclose materially different information, more sensitive information, or information for a different purpose.

(n) An Owner-authorized publication or distribution workflow may establish continuing authority to transmit specified classes of finished content to a designated external destination for the approved purpose only when that standing authorization is represented in current State.

(o) Authority under paragraph (n) is limited to the approved content class, destination, account or service, and purpose. It does not authorize transmission of unrelated project files, source material, credentials, logs, private records, intermediate artifacts, or other information not reasonably necessary for the publication.

(p) Where a publication workflow is automated, the system must use reasonable safeguards to confirm that the material selected for transmission belongs to the authorized publication class and destination before transmission occurs.

(q) Routine publication performed within a valid standing publication authorization represented in State does not require separate Owner approval for each individual upload unless the authorization expressly requires it.
